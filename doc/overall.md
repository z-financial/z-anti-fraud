Fraud prevention systems operate in a highly dynamic environment where attackers continuously adapt their behaviour to existing detection mechanisms. Rule-based approaches are valuable because they can respond quickly to newly identified fraud patterns, while machine-learning models can detect more complex behaviours. However, both approaches depend heavily on reliable ground-truth data, continuous monitoring, and feedback mechanisms, as rules can become outdated and models can degrade when fraud patterns change.

In many systems, fraud logic is implemented directly inside individual business services, causing rules to become scattered across login, payment, trading, wallet, and other workflows. This tightly couples fraud prevention with core business logic, making rules difficult to manage, update, audit, and reuse. It also increases the engineering effort required to introduce new detection strategies and raises the risk that fraud-related changes may affect critical transaction flows.

Therefore, this research investigates a dedicated and minimally invasive anti-fraud architecture that separates fraud decision-making from business services while integrating with them through well-defined interfaces and events. The proposed approach combines centralized rule evaluation, machine-learning scoring, manual review, monitoring, ground-truth collection, and continuous feedback, allowing fraud detection capabilities to evolve independently from the core financial system.
